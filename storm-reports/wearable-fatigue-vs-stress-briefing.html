01

The 60-Second Summary

The settled fact: heart-rate variability does shift with sleep loss, but it is not specific to it. It tracks autonomic arousal, so stress, posture, caffeine and workload move it the same way. When models are tested on people they never saw, accuracy collapses (one 86-driver real-road study fell from ~85% to 44%). That is exactly what the team's own data showed. What the field actually relies on is (1) sleep/wake history plus time of day, and (2) behaviour that degrades with sleepiness, such as eye closure (PERCLOS) or attention lapses on a reaction-time test. The contested part is whether any wearable physiology adds value on top of those. The one published real-road comparison found the jump came from adding driving time and day/night, not from better physiology. So the fix is not a better HRV classifier: make time awake and circadian phase the core of the score, add a cheap sleepiness-specific test, and use heart data only to adjust or veto per person, never to label someone "stressed". That last point is also a legal one: under the EU AI Act, inferring fatigue is allowed, but general stress monitoring of workers is not.

Five Key Findings, Ranked by Reliability

HRV moves with sleep loss, but it is inconsistent across studies and fails on unseen people
1
Reliability: High
8/10

A systematic review of 18 driver studies found inconsistent alert-vs-fatigued HRV differences and detection accuracy from 44% to 100%. A second review (19 studies, all observational, quality rated low) reported sensitivity of 47.1–95%. In the largest real-road study (86 drivers), random forest reached ~85% for two classes, but subject-independent accuracy dropped to 44%, and the authors call HRV alone a poor indicator. A 2025 meta-analysis (11 trials, n=549) found sleep deprivation raises LF and LF/HF, but the RMSSD drop was borderline (SMD −0.24) and heterogeneity was very high (I²=91%). Score capped at 8 because the studies are mostly simulator or lab work with small samples.

Inferring fatigue is legal under the EU AI Act; general stress monitoring of workers is not
2
Reliability: High
8/10

Article 5(1)(f) of the AI Act, applicable from 2 Feb 2025, bans emotion recognition in the workplace. The Commission's guidelines say fatigue is a physical state, not an emotion, and give inferring a pilot's or driver's fatigue as an example of what is "not emotion recognition". But "the general monitoring of stress levels at the workplace is not permitted under health or safety aspects" (para 257). Stress inference is only allowed where an elevated stress level would pose a specific danger, such as around dangerous machines. Separately, the EU General Safety Regulation made Driver Drowsiness and Attention Warning mandatory on all new vehicles registered from 7 July 2024. Score is 8, not higher, because these are guidelines, not court rulings.

Context (time awake, time of day) carries most of the predictive power
3
Reliability: Medium-High
7/10

In a 43-driver real-road study using consumer wearables, HRV alone gave AUC 0.76. Adding driving time and day/night raised it to 0.88, so the gain came from context, not from better physiology. This matches the team's DROZY result: hours awake alone scored 0.76 per session and 0.91 within each person, and adding the upgraded HRV features barely changed it (0.78). Trait-like individual differences in vulnerability to sleep loss (ICC 0.68–0.92) mean a population model will misjudge some individuals, so context sets the prior but cannot be the whole answer. Score is 7: one peer-reviewed study plus the team's own replication.

Reliability: Medium-High
7/10

In the FHWA/NHTSA study (14 people, 42 hours awake), only PERCLOS (the share of time the eyes are mostly closed) correlated highly with lapses on a reaction-time test (the psychomotor vigilance test, PVT), both within and between subjects. It beat two EEG algorithms, two blink monitors and self-rated sleepiness. Per-subject correlations averaged about 0.87 (n=10 scorable, range 0.55–0.97). The Australian aviation regulator's guidance on biomathematical fatigue models says model predictions "cannot form the sole means" for operational decisions. The polygraph is the cautionary parallel: the National Research Council concluded that arousal measures carry an "inherent ambiguity" that better technique can only modestly fix. Score is 7: a government report rather than a journal paper, with a small sample.

Contested signal · monitor, do not assert · confidence 4/10

Skin conductance (EDA) or skin temperature could separate stress from sleepiness

The idea is that stress raises skin conductance while sleepiness lowers arousal, and that distal skin warming precedes sleep onset. In the largest test (Kurinec et al. 2022, n=193, 38 hours of sleep deprivation), non-specific skin conductance responses did change with sleep loss, but those changes did not moderate the effect on sleepiness or vigilant attention. So EDA tracks sleep loss at group level, not individual impairment. The skin-temperature evidence (Kräuchi 1999/2000) concerns how fast people fall asleep in bed, not alertness at work. Worth testing as an extra input; do not claim it solves the stress confound.

Contested signal · vendor claims · confidence 3/10

"97%" and "98%" fatigue reductions from mining deployments

Caterpillar's Driver Safety System (DSS) figure at the PotashCorp Aurora mine (North Carolina) covers fatigue and distraction events combined, across a phased rollout, and comes only from trade press. Cat's own page gives 80% for average fatigue events. Optalert's 98% is a drop in alerts per operating hour at an unnamed mine, with no baseline. Do not quote either as independent evidence.

03

The Hidden Connection

On the surface the findings pull in different directions. The academic says HRV really does change with sleep loss, while the skeptic and the team's own data say it is useless for detection. The economist says buyers don't pay for accuracy, while the practitioner says detection only matters if someone responds to the alert.

All of these follow from one fact: HRV measures arousal, and fatigue is defined by sleep history and the body clock. Because HRV measures arousal, it fails on unseen people, since everyone's arousal baseline and stressors differ. For the same reason it becomes legally risky: a model that partly detects stress is close to banned workplace stress monitoring. The polygraph shows the same pattern: an arousal sensor cannot tell you why arousal changed. And hours awake wins because it is a direct measure of what fatigue actually is.

Make the clock the detector and the heart the referee: time awake and circadian phase decide the risk, a reaction-time test confirms impairment, and HRV only adjusts the score per person or explains it away.

The assumption this briefing rests on (and the missing 6th lens)

All five lenses assumed that "fatigue" means sleepiness from sleep loss, measured by self-report (KSS) or reaction-time lapses, and that the wearer will put the device on and accept being scored. None of them spoke for the worker wearing it. The missing sixth lens is the worker and their representatives: occupational health, unions, and the person who gets the alert. That lens decides whether the device is worn at all (practitioners report it is hard to get operators to wear devices every night), whether alerts are trusted or ignored, and whether escalation reads as care or as discipline.

The omission that could reverse these conclusions: if workers game or refuse a schedule-and-test system, a passive physiological signal might be the only data you get, even though it is weaker. Build with consent, worker-owned data, and "help, not punish" response rules from day one.

04

What To Actually Do Differently

For the BreathBreak team building on the TI AFE4950 sensor and SK-AM62 board: specific changes to the model, the device and the pitch.

01
Make a fatigue-risk score from hours awake, sleep in the last 24 hours, and time of day the core of the model.

Collect sleep and wake time with a two-tap check-in at shift start, plus the shift clock. Implement a simple two-process model: sleep pressure rising with time awake, plus a 24-hour circadian curve. On DROZY this information alone reached 0.91 within each person, versus 0.59 for the best HRV model. Findings 3 and 4.

02
Add a 2–3 minute reaction-time test (a PVT) on the SK-AM62 at shift start and at each break.

Count lapses (reactions slower than 500 ms). This is the behavioural ground truth that PERCLOS was validated against, it needs no camera, and it measures sleepiness rather than arousal. It also gives you labelled data to recalibrate each worker. Finding 4.

03
Demote HRV to a per-person modifier and a veto, and report its added value honestly.

Use the AFE4950 ECG to (a) lower the alert when vagal tone is normal for that person and (b) flag "something changed" for a follow-up PVT. The headline metric becomes: leave-one-subject-out gain over the schedule-only model, with WESAD stress as an explicit negative test. If the gain is zero, say so. That is what the 86-driver study found too. Findings 1 and 3.

04
Never output, display or log a "stress" score; output only fatigue risk.

General stress monitoring of workers is not permitted under the EU AI Act guidelines, while fatigue inference is not emotion recognition. Remove stress wording from the app, the slides and the stored data, and keep the HRV veto internal. Finding 2.

05
Design the response loop first: alert, breathing break, re-test, then optional escalation, with worker-visible logs.

The peer-reviewed deployment data (vendor-linked) suggests the response, not the sensor, drives the reduction: 66% with an in-cab alert, 94% with employer feedback. Buyers pay for the audit trail. Put the "help, not punish" rule in writing, as even vendor authors advise. Finding 5 and the 6th lens.

06
Tell the honest story on the slide: "HRV can't tell tired from stressed, so we don't ask it to."

Show the team's own result (HRV at chance, hours awake 0.76/0.91, stress false alarms 70–100%) next to Persson 2021 (85% falling to 44% on unseen drivers). A validated negative result, plus a design that answers it, is stronger than an inflated accuracy number. Finding 1.

What's Safe to Assert

✓ Safe — verified against primary source

  • A review of 18 studies found HRV-based driver fatigue detection accuracy ranging from 44% to 100% (Lu et al. 2022).
  • In an 86-driver real-road study, HRV accuracy was ~85% within-sample but 44% on unseen drivers (Persson et al. 2021).
  • Adding driving time and day/night raised wearable HRV detection from AUC 0.76 to 0.88 (Lu et al. 2022, IEEE T-ITS).
  • PERCLOS was the only measure tested that correlated highly with PVT lapses within and between subjects (FHWA 1998).
  • Under the EU AI Act guidelines, inferring fatigue is not emotion recognition; general workplace stress monitoring is not permitted.
  • Driver drowsiness warning has been mandatory on all new EU vehicle registrations since 7 July 2024.
  • Seeing Machines: FY2025 revenue US$62.3M, adjusted EBITDA loss US$30.1M.

⚠ Say with a caveat

  • Sleep deprivation raises LF/HF and lowers RMSSD (meta-analysis; RMSSD effect borderline, very high heterogeneity).
  • Vulnerability to sleep loss is trait-like (ICC 0.68–0.92; one study, n=21).
  • Feedback to the employer raised fatigue-event reduction from 66% to 94% (peer-reviewed but vendor-linked, single company).
  • Oura ~$11B valuation (company announcement; revenue press-reported). NSC: fatigue costs ~$1.4M per year per 1,000 employees (2017 calculator).
  • Biomathematical models are validated at population level only and should not be the sole basis for decisions (CASA 2010).

✕ Don't assert

  • "HRV detects fatigue with 85–100% accuracy" (within-sample or best-case figures only).
  • Caterpillar "97%" or Optalert "98%" as independent evidence (vendor or trade press; different metrics).
  • "$1,200–3,100 per employee" fatigue cost (no primary source found).
  • "Skin conductance or temperature separates stress from fatigue" (group-level only; not validated for individuals).
  • That BreathBreak measures or detects stress (legal exposure, and the team's own data says it is a confound).
05

The Frontier Question

Does any wearable signal add predictive value on top of a sleep-history and circadian model, when tested on unseen people with stress as an explicit negative condition?

Every lens circled this question without asking it directly. If the answer is no, a fatigue wearable is really a scheduling and testing tool with a sensor attached, and the hardware's value is getting the device worn and the alert answered. If the answer is yes for some signal (personalised HRV, PVT plus HRV, or a specific sleepiness marker), that signal is the product. It is cheap to test: the team already has DROZY (sleep loss), WESAD (stress) and a working leave-one-subject-out pipeline. On DROZY the first answer was "barely" (0.76 to 0.78), but with 11 people and 29 sessions that is far from settled.
